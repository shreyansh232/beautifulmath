## First principles: an operation is a verb

A number sits still. An **operation** does something to it.

Think of a machine with an input and an output:

$$
\text{input} \xrightarrow{\text{operation}} \text{output}
$$

Addition by $5$ sends every number $x$ to $x + 5$. Multiplication by $3$ sends $x$ to $3x$. These are **transformations** of the number line (or of a collection’s size).

School often teaches operations as procedures to execute on worksheets. The first-principles view is simpler and deeper: **operations are structured ways the world (or a model) can change a quantity.**

## Addition: combining

If you have a pile of $a$ and a pile of $b$ of the same kind, their combine-count is $a + b$. Addition is the transformation that merges same-kind amounts.

Properties that fall out of the idea (for ordinary numbers):

- **Commutative:** $a + b = b + a$ — order of merging does not matter.
- **Associative:** $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ — grouping does not matter.
- **Identity:** $a + 0 = a$ — merging with nothing changes nothing.

Subtraction is not a new alien species. It is asking for the **undo** of an addition: $x + 5 = 12$ means $x = 12 - 5$. On the integers, every addition by $k$ has an inverse addition by $-k$.

> Why does this work? Because addition by $k$ is a shift, and shifts can be shifted back.

## Multiplication: scaling (and more)

For whole numbers, $3 \times 4$ can be read as three copies of four:

$$
4 + 4 + 4 = 12
$$

That is a valid model — and incomplete as a final story. Multiplication is better seen as **scaling** or **combining factors**:

- Stretch a length by $3$.
- Form an area: width times height.
- Convert units: $2$ hours $\times$ $60$ minutes/hour.

Arrays show why $3 \times 4 = 4 \times 3$: rearrange the grid and the count is unchanged. That is commutativity with a picture.

Division asks: **what input, after scaling by $k$, produces this output?** If $k \neq 0$, multiplication by $k$ is invertible, and division is that inverse.

$$
\text{If } y = kx \text{ and } k \neq 0, \text{ then } x = \frac{y}{k}.
$$

Division by zero has no inverse story: multiplication by $0$ crushes every input to $0$, so you cannot recover which input you started from.

## Structure over memorization

Times tables are useful muscle memory. They are not the concept. The concept is:

1. What transformation am I applying?
2. Does it have an inverse?
3. What stays invariant? (Commutativity, associativity, identities.)

When algebra arrives, you will move symbols using these same laws. When calculus arrives, derivatives will describe rates of transformations. When linear algebra arrives, matrices will package many multiply-and-add transformations at once.

## Real world: recipes, maps, and money

Double a recipe: multiply every ingredient by $2$. Halve it: multiply by $\tfrac{1}{2}$ (or divide by $2$). Convert $\$50$ at $83$ rupees per dollar: multiply. Each is the same verb — scale — wearing different clothes.

If you scale and want the original, you apply the inverse scale. That is not a trick; it is the definition of undoing.

## Mathematician: passion for numerical structure

Ramanujan’s notebooks (compiled largely before he left for Cambridge) hold on the order of three thousand results. He often recorded statements without proofs — influenced in style by G. S. Carr’s *Synopsis*, which listed theorem after theorem. Friends and later mathematicians spent decades proving and clarifying what he saw.

The usable ethic for a learner rebuilding foundations is not mysticism. It is this: **treat operations and patterns as worthy of obsession.** Ask what remains true when you rearrange, invert, or specialize. Curiosity is a work habit.

## AI / ML bridge: multiply, add, reverse

At the smallest scale, a neuron computes a weighted sum — multiplications and additions — then applies a nonlinearity. Training asks how a tiny change in a weight would change the loss, which means tracing those multiplications backward. You will meet this formally as the chain rule. For now, keep the intuition: **learning systems are huge webs of invertible and non-invertible numerical transformations.**

## Carry this forward

- Operations transform quantities.
- Subtraction and division (nonzero) are undoing stories.
- Multiplication is scaling and structure, not merely “repeated addition forever.”

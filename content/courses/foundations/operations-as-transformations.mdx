## First principles: an operation is a verb

A number sits still. An **operation** does something to it.

Think of a machine with an input and an output:

$$
\text{input} \xrightarrow{\text{operation}} \text{output}
$$

Addition by $5$ sends every number $x$ to $x + 5$. Multiplication by $3$ sends $x$ to $3x$. These are **transformations** of quantity.

School often teaches operations as procedures on worksheets. The first-principles view is simpler: **operations are structured ways the world (or a model) can change a quantity.**


## Slow down: undo as superpower

Solving equations is mostly invertibility with manners: do the same to both sides, using operations that can be undone. Addition undoes by subtraction. Multiplication undoes by division when the factor is nonzero. Keep asking "can I reverse this step?"

## Addition: combining

If you have a pile of $a$ and a pile of $b$ of the same kind, their combine-count is $a + b$.

Properties that fall out of the idea:

- **Commutative:** $a + b = b + a$
- **Associative:** $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$
- **Identity:** $a + 0 = a$

Subtraction asks for the **undo** of an addition. On the integers, every addition by $k$ has an inverse.

> Why does this work? Because addition by $k$ is a shift, and shifts can be shifted back.

## Multiplication: scaling (and more)

For whole numbers, $3 \times 4$ can be read as three copies of four. Better: see it as **scaling** and **structure**.

Arrays show why $3 \times 4 = 4 \times 3$: rearrange the grid and the count is unchanged.

Division asks: **what input, after scaling by $k$, produces this output?** If $k \neq 0$, multiplication by $k$ is invertible.

$$
\text{If } y = kx \text{ and } k \neq 0, \text{ then } x = \frac{y}{k}.
$$

Division by zero has no inverse story: multiplication by $0$ crushes every input to $0$.

## Structure over memorization

Times tables are useful muscle memory. They are not the concept. Ask:

1. What transformation am I applying?
2. Does it have an inverse?
3. What stays invariant?

When algebra arrives, you move symbols using these laws. When linear algebra arrives, matrices package many multiply-and-add transformations at once.

## Real world

Double a recipe: multiply by $2$. Halve it: multiply by $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Convert currency: multiply by a rate. Each is the same verb - scale - wearing different clothes.

## AI / ML bridge

At the smallest scale, a neuron computes a weighted sum - multiplications and additions - then applies a nonlinearity. Training traces those multiplications backward. For now: **learning systems are webs of numerical transformations**, some invertible, some not.

## First principles: name before you know

Sometimes you know the relationship before you know the number.

You know the room’s perimeter is $2(L + W)$ before you know $L$. You know a fair split of a bill before you know each person’s share. A **variable** is a **name** for a quantity that can take different values — or whose value is not yet determined in the problem.

That sounds almost too simple. It is the entire gateway to algebra.

Without names, every problem is a pile of specific arithmetic. With names, you can write a **pattern** once and reuse it:

$$
A = \pi r^2
$$

Here $r$ varies; the relationship does not.

## Unknowns versus placeholders

Two closely related uses:

1. **Unknown:** find the number $x$ such that $2x + 3 = 11$.
2. **General quantity:** for every real $x$, $x + 0 = x$.

Same letters, different jobs. Beginners get hurt when teachers switch jobs silently. Ask which job a letter is doing.

## Equations as truth conditions

An equation is a claim that two expressions name the same amount. Solving means finding values that make the claim true.

Example:

$$
2x + 3 = 11
$$

Undo transformations from the outside in — the invertibility lesson from operations:

1. Subtract $3$ from both sides: $2x = 8$.
2. Divide by $2$: $x = 4$.

You preserved truth at each step because you applied the **same** invertible operation to both sides.

> Why does this work? An equation asserts equality. If two amounts are equal, transforming both by the same invertible map keeps them equal. That is balancing.

## al-Khwārizmī: restoration and balancing

Al-Khwārizmī’s algebra text teaches methods later summarized as:

- **al-jabr (restoration):** repairing an equation by moving terms — historically associated with eliminating subtraction by “restoring” amounts.
- **al-muqābala (balancing):** simplifying by canceling like terms on opposite sides.

He reduced problems to canonical forms and explained solutions with arithmetic and geometric reasoning. Historians debate how directly he used Euclid; what matters for learning is the ethic: **make a repeatable method for isolating the unknown.**

Note what he did *not* yet have in modern form: the fully symbolic letter-algebra of later European mathematics. The conceptual leap — treat the unknown systematically — is already there.

## Real world

Engineers write $F = ma$ before a particular force is measured. Pharmacists write dose $= c \cdot w$ with body weight $w$ variable across patients. Programmers write `total = price * quantity` with different carts. Naming is leverage.

## AI / ML bridge: features and parameters

A supervised learning problem is full of named quantities:

- $x^{(i)}$ — the features of example $i$
- $y^{(i)}$ — the target
- $w$ — parameters (weights) the algorithm will adjust

A linear model predicts $\hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$. Training searches for $w$ and $b$ that make $\hat{y}$ close to $y$ on average. The childhood move “let $x$ be the unknown” becomes “let $w$ be what we learn.”

Same naming power. Larger stage.

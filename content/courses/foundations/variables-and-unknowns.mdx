## First principles: name before you know

Sometimes you know the relationship before you know the number.

You know the room’s perimeter is $2(L + W)$ before you know $L$. A **variable** is a **name** for a quantity that can take different values - or whose value is not yet determined.

Without names, every problem is a pile of specific arithmetic. With names, you write a **pattern** once:

$$
A = \pi r^2
$$

Here $r$ varies; the relationship does not.

## Unknowns versus placeholders

Two closely related jobs:

1. **Unknown:** find $x$ such that $2x + 3 = 11$.
2. **General quantity:** for every real $x$, $x + 0 = x$.

Ask which job a letter is doing.

## Equations as truth conditions

An equation claims two expressions name the same amount. Solving finds values that make the claim true.

$$
2x + 3 = 11
$$

<BalanceDiagram />

Undo transformations from the outside in:

1. Subtract $3$ from both sides: $2x = 8$.
2. Divide by $2$: $x = 4$.

You preserved truth because you applied the **same** invertible operation to both sides.

> Why does this work? If two amounts are equal, transforming both by the same invertible map keeps them equal. That is balancing.

## al-Khwārizmī: restoration and balancing

His algebra text teaches methods later summarized as:

- **al-jabr (restoration):** repairing an equation by moving terms.
- **al-muqābala (balancing):** canceling like terms on opposite sides.

He reduced problems to canonical forms with arithmetic and geometric reasoning. He did not yet have modern letter-algebra; the leap is treating the unknown systematically.

## Real world

Engineers write $F = ma$ before a particular force is measured. Pharmacists write dose $= c \cdot w$ with weight $w$ variable. Naming is leverage.

## AI / ML bridge

A supervised problem is full of named quantities: $x^{(i)}$ features, $y^{(i)}$ targets, $w$ parameters. A linear model predicts $\hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$. Training searches for $w$ and $b$. The childhood move “let $x$ be the unknown” becomes “let $w$ be what we learn.”

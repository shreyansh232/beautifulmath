## First principles

One equation in two unknowns usually has many solutions (a whole line of them). To pin both unknowns, you typically need **two independent equations**.

Example:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x + y &= 10 \\
2x - y &= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Add them: $3x = 12$, so $x = 4$, then $y = 6$.

You eliminated a variable by combining truth claims. That is the heart of elimination methods associated with Gauss's style of systematic reduction.

## Geometric picture

Each linear equation in two variables is a line. The solution is their intersection.

- One intersection: unique solution
- Parallel distinct lines: no solution (inconsistent)
- Same line twice: infinitely many solutions

## Independence matters

If the second equation is just a double of the first, you learned nothing new. Independence means each equation adds information the others do not already contain.

## Real world

Two price constraints fix two item prices. One constraint leaves a free family of possibilities.

## AI / ML bridge

With many data points you get many approximate equations for few weights. Ordinary least squares finds the compromise that minimizes squared residuals. Later linear algebra will say this with matrices in one line: $Xw \approx y$.

## How this idea was born

Wind has speed and direction. A force pushes a certain amount along a heading. Displacement says how far and which way you walked. For centuries, physics needed a citizen that could carry both magnitude and direction without collapsing into a single bare number.

**Josiah Willard Gibbs** (1839-1903), with Heaviside and others, helped push vector language into a form physicists could compute with, amid competing formalisms. MacTutor sketches the career. Steal the ethic: choose representations that make the next calculation inevitable, not ornamental. As an arrow a vector knows direction and length; as a list it knows components along axes. That dual view is why the same language powers geometry, physics, and data tables.

This chapter is the foundation for a modern rereader before matrices as maps, alignment via dot products, and bases as scaffolding. If you only memorize column notation, you miss the geometry. If you only draw arrows, you cannot scale to a million features.

## First principles

A **vector** can be:

1. A geometric arrow (direction + length)
2. A coordinate list $(v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n)$

<VectorDiagram />

Addition tip-to-tail matches componentwise sums. Scaling $c\mathbf{v}$ stretches (or flips if $c < 0$). The zero vector is the arrow of no displacement and the list of all zeros.

Linear combinations $a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{w}$ fill a line or a plane (or more) depending on how independent $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ are. That is a preview of span; the bases chapter will make independence precise.

## Worked intuition

Tip-to-tail addition is a physical story: walk one displacement, then another. The shortcut home is the sum. Componentwise addition is the same story told in coordinates. When data scientists say "vector," they often mean the coordinate list - but the arrow intuition still teaches what scaling and adding do.

If $\mathbf{u}$ points east and $\mathbf{v}$ points north, $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$ points northeast with a length the Pythagorean theorem can compute. Geometry lesson, still paying rent.

One row of a spreadsheet is a vector. Features become axes. Distance and angle become geometry on data. Multiplying by $-1$ reverses direction. Multiplying by $2$ doubles length without turning (in the usual Euclidean picture).

## Common confusions

**Speed without direction.** A weather report that says "wind 40" without a heading is half a vector. Sailors, pilots, and robots need the missing half. Data science makes the same demand: a feature without an axis meaning is a number adrift.

**Adding vectors from different spaces.** You can add two length-3 lists only when corresponding components mean the same axes. Age plus income is meaningful as a feature vector only after you decide those are coordinates of one record, not after you pretend the units magically match for every operation.

**Position versus free vector.** In geometry pedagogy, arrows can slide (free vectors) or be anchored at the origin as position vectors. The algebra is related; the story differs. Be explicit which picture you are using.

**"High-dimensional" as mystical.** Dimension $n$ means $n$ independent axes in the list view. It is bookkeeping with geometry attached, not a portal to another metaphysics.

**Treating one-hot IDs as geometrically meaningful distances.** Index $7$ is not "near" index $8$ in meaning just because the integers are neighbors. Embedding spaces try to fix that; raw IDs do not.

**Forgetting origin dependence in some applied settings.** Physics free vectors slide; some data vectors are anchored interpretations. State your picture.

## Real world
Wind has speed and direction. A force pushes a certain amount along a heading. Displacements add as vectors: walk east then north; net displacement is the diagonal. Robotics, navigation, and animation stacks live on these additions all day.

A data record with age, income, and score is a vector in feature space. Once axes are fixed, "nearby" becomes a geometric question.

## AI / ML bridge

Every embedding, weight row, and activation is a vector. Similarity search is often nearest neighbors in vector space. Word embeddings place tokens in high-dimensional space so "direction" can encode relations. The famous king - man + woman ≈ queen analogy is vector arithmetic on meaning (imperfect, but the geometry is the point).

You will soon multiply vectors by matrices and score alignment with dot products. Those operations only make sense because vectors are first-class objects with addition and scaling laws.

## A longer geometric studio

Draw two arrows tip-to-tail on paper. Draw the diagonal. Then write their components and add componentwise. Confirm the diagonal matches. That dual check - geometry then coordinates - is the dual citizen habit this chapter wants.

Scale a vector by $2$, by $1/2$, by $-1$. Watch length and direction respond. Negative scaling is a reverse, not a mystical "negative arrow" separate from geometry.

Take a spreadsheet row: age, hours studied, score. Treat it as a vector only after you decide those axes. Then ask what adding two students' rows would mean. Sometimes it means something (total hours). Sometimes it is nonsense (average ages mixed carelessly). Axes discipline is data ethics in miniature.

## Span without the full theory

Fix two nonparallel arrows in the plane. Their combinations $a\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{w}$ fill the plane. Fix two parallel arrows; combinations fill only a line. Independence is hiding in that contrast. The bases chapter will name it. Already you can feel redundancy versus freedom.

## Embeddings with humility

King - man + woman ≈ queen is a famous demo of directional structure in embedding space. It is imperfect and corpus-dependent. The point is not the party trick. The point is that meaning can acquire geometry when you commit to vectors. Steal the geometry; keep humility about the demos.

## Extended examples

**Displacement story.** Walk $\mathbf{u}=(3,0)$ then $\mathbf{v}=(0,4)$. Sum $(3,4)$ with length $5$. Pythagoras pays rent inside vector addition.

**Feature row discipline.** Student A: $(age=20, hours=5)$. Student B: $(21, 7)$. Sum $(41, 12)$ might mean a combined study group total hours with a nonsense combined age. Decide per coordinate whether addition is meaningful.

**Scaling in embeddings.** Multiplying an embedding by $10$ changes Euclidean nearest neighbors more than cosine neighbors. Geometry choice changes conclusions.

**Zero vector role.** Identity for addition; the "do nothing" displacement. In equations, $\mathbf{v}+(-\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{0}$ is the reversible move ethic from algebra returning in vector clothes.

**Dual view drill.** For each example this week, say aloud: "arrow story" then "list story." Dual fluency is the chapter's real exam.

## Glossary of moves

- **Arrow view:** direction plus length.
- **List view:** ordered components on axes.
- **Addition:** tip-to-tail / componentwise.
- **Scaling:** stretch or reverse.
- **Linear combination:** weighted sum of vectors.
- **Span (preview):** all combinations of a set.
- **Zero vector:** additive identity.

## One more vignette

A pilot cares about wind as a vector because half information kills. Data scientists rediscover the same lesson when a feature column lacks an axis story. Whenever someone hands you a list of numbers and says "it's a vector," ask what each component points along. The question is not pedantry. It is how geometry stays honest inside tables.

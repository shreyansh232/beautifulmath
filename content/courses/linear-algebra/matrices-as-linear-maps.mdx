## How this idea was born

Tables of numbers show up everywhere: inventories, pixel grids, systems of coefficients. The human struggle is what those tables **do**. Stretch, rotate, shear, mix features, send one list of numbers to another in a disciplined way. A matrix is not a spreadsheet that happens to be square. It is a machine.

**Arthur Cayley** (1821-1895) developed matrix algebra as an object of study in its own right, not merely a bookkeeping grid. MacTutor sketches the prolific career. Steal the ethic: when a notation keeps appearing, promote it to a citizen of mathematics and learn its laws.

A modern campfire story sits in Strogatz's "Untangling the Web": Google's **PageRank**. A page's importance depends on the importance of pages that link to it. Ranking the web becomes finding structure in a giant link matrix, not reading every page by hand. Treat that as popular pedagogy about matrices-as-machines, not as a full corporate history.

For a modern rereader, this chapter rebuilds the machine view so later lessons on projections and bases feel inevitable rather than bolted on.

## First principles

A map $T$ is **linear** if

$$
T(a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{v}) = a T(\mathbf{u}) + b T(\mathbf{v})
$$

It respects addition and scaling. No curving, no shifting by itself (a pure translation is affine, not linear).

A matrix $A$ represents a linear map via $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x}$.

<MatrixMapDiagram />

Columns of $A$ are where the basis vectors go. That one sentence unlocks most geometric matrix intuition. $A\mathbf{x}$ is a linear combination of $A$'s columns with weights from $\mathbf{x}$.

Composition: doing $B$ then $A$ is the product $AB$ (order matters). Neural nets stack such maps, then add nonlinearities between them.

## Worked intuition

If you know where $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ go, you know the whole linear map on the plane - because every vector is a combo of those basis arrows. Matrix columns are those destinations. That sentence is worth a week of homework.

Watch a unit square under a matrix. Rotation turns it. Shear tilts it. Scaling stretches sides. Projection flattens it onto a lower-dimensional shadow. Ask of every matrix: what does it do to the square?

Algebrica's matrices and linear maps pages show arrays becoming transformations. Watch where basis vectors go - that habit unlocks most geometric matrix intuition.

In data language, a weight matrix learns which linear mixture of input features predicts well. The geometry and the spreadsheet are the same object wearing two outfits.

## Common confusions

**Order of multiplication.** Doing shear then rotate is not rotate then shear. $AB$ and $BA$ usually differ. Write the maps in the order they act, carefully.

**Linearity versus affine.** $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ is affine when $\mathbf{b} \neq 0$. Neural layers are affine maps plus a nonlinearity. People say "linear layer" colloquially; the bias term already left pure linearity.

**Matrices as only "numbers in a grid."** Without the map story, multiplication rules feel arbitrary. With the map story, multiplication is composition, and the rules become inevitable.

**Invertibility as default.** Many matrices squash space (singular maps). Not every transformation undoes. Rank (coming in the bases chapter) measures how many independent output directions survive.

**Assuming invertibility.** Many maps lose information. Solving $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ may have zero or infinitely many solutions.

**Reading every array as a transform.** Some matrices are mere data tables. The map story applies when multiplication means applying a linear action.

## Real world
Image transforms, robot joint kinematics (linearized near an operating point), and spreadsheet "multiply this grid by that grid" workflows are matrix moves. Computer graphics pipelines are long compositions of linear and affine maps.

Whenever a system mixes inputs into outputs with constant coefficients, a matrix is waiting to be named.

## AI / ML bridge

A dense neural layer is literally $\mathbf{x} \mapsto W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$. Almost every classical ML model has a matrix at its core: linear regression, PCA loadings, Markov transition matrices, attention's query-key-value projections.

Attention and convolutions are structured linear maps with weight sharing. The structure is engineering; the linearity is the mathematical spine.
</LaterBridge>

## For the rereader

After bases and rank, return and reinterpret columns as spanning the column space. After probability, notice that a transition matrix for a Markov chain is a linear map on distribution vectors (with extra constraints: nonnegative entries, columns or rows summing to one, depending on convention).

A sharper nuance: nonlinearity is what makes deep nets expressive beyond stacked linear maps. Without nonlinearities, a deep stack of matrices collapses to one matrix. The "depth" miracle requires breaking linearity between layers - which makes the linear pieces even more important to understand cleanly.

## Affine honesty

Neural "linear layers" often mean $\mathbf{x}\mapsto W\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}$. The bias makes them affine. Stack affine maps and you still have an affine map. Nonlinearities between layers are what make depth expressive. Understanding the linear pieces cleanly makes the nonlinear miracle easier to see.

## Extended examples

**Projection matrix (rank one).** $A=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^T/(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u})$ (column vector conventions) projects onto $\mathbf{u}$'s direction. Multiplying by $A$ is a map; the formula ties this lesson to the next.

**Rotation by $90^\circ$.** Columns land at $(0,1)$ and $(-1,0)$ depending on orientation convention. Squares turn; lengths stay. Orthogonal matrices are length-preserving linear maps - a preview.

**Shear.** $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ tilts vertical lines. Area can stay while shape changes. Ask what is preserved, not only what moves.

**Composition bug hunt.** Implement $AB\mathbf{x}$ versus $BA\mathbf{x}$ numerically on a random $\mathbf{x}$. When they differ, celebrate: the machine view is working.

**Neural depth collapse.** Without nonlinearities, $W_3W_2W_1$ is one matrix. Depth needs breaks in linearity. Understand the linear bricks so the mortar (activations) makes sense.

## Glossary of moves

- **Linearity:** respects addition and scaling.
- **Matrix-vector product:** linear combination of columns.
- **Columns-as-images:** where basis vectors land.
- **Composition:** multiply matrices; order matters.
- **Affine map:** linear map plus translation.
- **Singular map:** loses dimensions; not invertible.
- **Rank (preview):** dimension of column space.

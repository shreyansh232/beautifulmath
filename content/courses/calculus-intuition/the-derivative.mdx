## First principles

If $f$ is smooth enough,

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

is the slope at $x = a$. The function $f'$ sends each $a$ to that slope.

Leibniz wrote $\frac{dy}{dx}$ to suggest a ratio of infinitesimal changes. Modern rigor uses limits; the intuition remains: tiny rise over tiny run.


## Slow down: a machine for slopes

The derivative $f'$ is itself a function: feed it a location, get the slope there. That shift - from one slope number to a slope machine - is what makes calculus compositional and what makes gradient learning possible later.

## Rules worth knowing early

- $(cf)' = c f'$
- $(f+g)' = f' + g'$
- $(x^n)' = n x^{n-1}$
- Chain rule: $(f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x)) g'(x)$

The chain rule is the star for ML: change through a pipeline multiplies local sensitivities.

## Linear approximation

Near $a$,

$$
f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)
$$

The derivative is the best local linear stand-in.

## Real world

If profit $P(q)$ depends on quantity $q$, $P'(q)$ is marginal profit at that scale.

## AI / ML bridge

Gradient descent steps opposite the gradient of loss. Each partial derivative asks: if I nudge this weight, how does loss respond?

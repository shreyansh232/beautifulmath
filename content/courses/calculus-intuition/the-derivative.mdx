## How this idea was born

Once you can ask how fast something changes at a single moment, a second need appears: a portable report of that steepness **everywhere**. Sensitivity analysis, orbits, and later machine learning all want a machine that, given a location, returns the local rate. One slope number is a reading. A slope-valued function is a dashboard.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646-1716) chased a universal symbolic language. Independent of Newton, he built a calculus whose form invited others to compute. MacTutor's Leibniz biography is the short reference; the Newton-Leibniz priority dispute is real history, not a classroom cartoon. Steal the ethic: design notation so future minds can carry the idea without you in the room. His tiny-change and sum symbols survived because they make reasoning about rates and accumulations feel almost mechanical.

For a modern rereader, that shift - from one number to a slope-valued function - is what makes calculus compositional, and why later gradient learning can ask for sensitivities everywhere at once. Good symbols are not cosmetics.

## First principles

If $f$ is smooth enough,

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

is the slope at $x = a$. The function $f'$ sends each $a$ to that slope (wherever the limit exists).

Leibniz wrote $\frac{dy}{dx}$ to suggest a ratio of infinitesimal changes. Modern rigor uses limits; the intuition remains: tiny rise over tiny run. Treat the fraction as a mnemonic with a deep idea inside, not as a license to cancel symbols carelessly in every context.

<LimitZoomDiagram />

The derivative is the best local linear stand-in. Near $a$,

$$
f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)
$$

That approximation is why derivatives power sensitivity analysis: a small nudge $\Delta x$ predicts a change about $f'(a)\Delta x$.

## Worked intuition

Think of $f'$ as a dashboard. Feed it $x = 2$ and read the slope there. Feed it $x = 5$ and read a different slope. The original $f$ is the landscape; $f'$ is the instrument panel that reports steepness at each mile marker.

Rules worth knowing early (after the picture is solid):

- $(cf)' = c f'$
- $(f+g)' = f' + g'$
- $(x^n)' = n x^{n-1}$
- Chain rule: $(f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x))\, g'(x)$

The chain rule is the star for machine learning: change through a pipeline multiplies local sensitivities. If loss depends on prediction, prediction on hidden layer, hidden layer on weights, the total sensitivity is a product of local slopes along the path.

Algebrica's derivatives page lists rules after the definition. Learn the definition's picture first; rules are speed, not meaning.

Ask of every smooth graph: where is $f'=0$, where is $|f'|$ large, and where is $f'$ undefined? Those questions are already optimization and robustness in miniature.

## Common confusions

**Derivative as "the formula" rather than a function.** People memorize $(x^2)'=2x$ and forget that $2x$ is itself a function reporting slopes. The mental shift matters when you differentiate compositions and when you later pack many partials into a gradient.

**Notation worship without meaning.** $\frac{dy}{dx}$ looks like a fraction because the intuition is fractional. It is not a free pass to treat differentials as ordinary numbers in every manipulation. Limits keep the story honest.

**$f'(a)=0$ means "nothing is happening."** On a smooth graph, zero slope means flatness at that point: a local max, min, or shelf. The function value can still be large; the rate is what vanished.

**Two formulas with the same derivative.** If $F'=G'$, then $F$ and $G$ differ by a constant (on an interval, under standard hypotheses). That is a preview of antiderivative families, not a paradox.

**Higher derivatives as decoration.** $f''$ is the rate of the rate. It is not fancier for its own sake; it measures how slope itself changes. Concavity stories live here.

**Differentiability implying nice global optimization.** Local slope zero is not global optimality on nonconvex landscapes. The derivative is local by nature.

## Real world
Sensitivity analysis asks: how much does output move when an input nudges? That is a derivative when the relationship is smooth. If profit $P(q)$ depends on quantity $q$, then $P'(q)$ is marginal profit at that scale. Engineers linearize complicated maps near an operating point for the same reason: the derivative is the trustworthy local translator.

## AI / ML bridge

Gradients are derivatives, or their multivariable cousins. Gradient descent steps opposite the gradient of loss. Each partial derivative asks: if I nudge this weight, how does loss respond?

Backpropagation is organized bookkeeping for the chain rule through a network. You do not need the implementation details yet. You need the moral: composition of functions becomes multiplication of local sensitivities, and that multiplication is what makes deep models trainable in principle.

## For the rereader

After integrals and gradients, return and reread the linear approximation line. Almost every "first-order" method in optimization and numerical analysis is that sentence in different clothes.

A sharper nuance: differentiability is a local property with global consequences for optimization. Nonsmooth points (ReLU kinks, absolute values) are not exotic edge cases in modern ML; they are everyday terrain. The classical derivative still teaches you what you are approximating when you use subgradients or smoothed surrogates.

Also notice how much pedagogy fails by teaching rules before the slope machine idea. If you ever teach this material, reverse that order on purpose.

On a third visit, derive $(fg)'$ and $(f/g)'$ from the limit definition once, slowly. Even if you forget the algebra later, you will remember that rules are earned. Then reread Leibniz's ethic: notation that carries ideas without you in the room. Ask whether your own notes meet that bar.

Keep a page of linear approximations you actually use: small percentage changes, rule-of-thumb sensitivities, "about how much if I nudge." Label each with the $f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$ pattern.

## A longer numerical studio

Pick $f(x)=x^3$ and compute $f'(2)$ from the limit definition with a shrinking $h$. Then use the power rule and confirm you land on $12$. The agreement is the point: rules are compressed limits, not a second religion.

Next, differentiate $f(g(x))$ with $f(u)=u^2$ and $g(x)=\sin x$ symbolically via the chain rule, then check numerically at a point by finite differences on the composition. Watching the product of local slopes match the overall difference quotient is the chain rule becoming bodily knowledge.

Linear approximation deserves a studio of its own. At $a=1$ for $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, compare $f(1.05)$ to $f(1)+f'(1)(0.05)$. Feel the error shrink as the nudge shrinks. That error story is why first-order methods dominate early optimization.

## Notation as ethics

Leibniz wanted symbols that think with you. $\frac{dy}{dx}$ remembers the tiny-ratio intuition. Newton's dot notation remembered fluxions in time. Modern $f'$ remembers the slope machine. None is sacred; each is a bet about what you want to see.

When you write $\frac{dy}{dx}$, ask whether you are treating it as a mnemonic, as a limit, or as a formal differential. Sliding between those readings without noticing is a common source of algebraic comedy. Keep the reading labeled in your head.

## Sensitivity in the wild

Engineers linearize around operating points because the derivative is the best local translator. A small change in input predicts a change $f'(a)\Delta x$ in output. Controllers, approximate models, and "what-if" spreadsheets all lean on that sentence.

If profit $P(q)$ depends on quantity, $P'(q)$ is marginal profit. If a loss $L(w)$ depends on a weight, $L'(w)$ (or a partial) is the training signal's one-dimensional ancestor. Same mathematics; different stakes.

## Extended examples

**Power rule from the definition (sketch).** For $f(x)=x^2$, expand $(x+h)^2-x^2=2xh+h^2$, divide by $h$, send $h\to0$, land on $2x$. The leftover $h$ term vanishing is the whole drama of the limit.

**Chain rule in a pipeline.** Let prediction $\hat{y}=w\cdot x$ and loss $L=(\hat{y}-y)^2$. Then $\frac{dL}{dw}=\frac{dL}{d\hat{y}}\frac{d\hat{y}}{dw}$. Two local sensitivities, one product. Deep networks stack more factors; the algebra does not change in spirit.

**Zero derivative checklist.** On a smooth closed interval, extrema occur where $f'=0$ or at endpoints (standard theorem hypotheses). A flat inflection can also show $f'=0$ without a local max/min. Sign charts save you from overclaiming.

**Nonsmooth preview.** $f(x)=|x|$ fails at $0$. ReLU activations import that failure into networks. Subgradients and smoothing are later tools; the classical derivative still teaches the ideal you approximate.

**Linearization error.** The difference between $f(a+h)$ and $f(a)+f'(a)h$ shrinks faster than $h$ for smooth enough $f$. That little-o story is why first-order steps work in a neighborhood and fail when you leap too far.

## Glossary of moves

- **Difference quotient:** average rate on an interval.
- **Derivative at a point:** limiting difference quotient.
- **Derivative as function:** $x\mapsto f'(x)$.
- **Linearization:** best local straight stand-in.
- **Chain rule:** multiply local sensitivities.
- **Critical point:** where $f'=0$ or fails to exist (context-dependent usage).
- **Smooth enough:** hypotheses that make theorems true; not a vibe.

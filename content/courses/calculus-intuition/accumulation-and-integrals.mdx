## How this idea was born

People needed **areas of curves** and totals built from rates long before school named the integral: distance from a changing speed, grain from a varying density, the region under a bow-shaped boundary that refuses to be a tidy rectangle. Derivatives measure change. Accumulation rebuilds the total.

Long before formal calculus, **Archimedes** exhausted areas and volumes with relentless approximation: trap the unknown between shapes he could control. MacTutor's biography is the standard entry. Steal the ethic: patience with limits. Do not demand the whole answer on the first polygon. Squeeze until only one possibility remains. Centuries later, integral calculus industrializes that boldness with systematic sums and a handshake with differentiation.

For a modern rereader, this chapter teaches you to rebuild totals from rates, then meets the fundamental theorem that makes calculus one subject rather than two clever tricks. Without that link, you have techniques. With it, you have a closed loop of ideas.

## First principles

Slice an interval into thin widths $\Delta x$, take height about $f(x_i)$, and form area about $f(x_i)\Delta x$. Sum the slices. As slices thin, the sum approaches the **definite integral** $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ when the limit exists.

<AreaUnderCurveDiagram />

If $f$ is a rate, the integral accumulates net change. Signed area matters: when $f$ dips below zero, contributions subtract. Direction and deficit are not decoration.

A function $F$ with $F' = f$ is an **antiderivative** of $f$. Adding a constant does not change the derivative, so antiderivatives come in families $F + C$.

**Fundamental theorem (intuition):** differentiating an accumulation from a fixed start recovers the rate; integrating a rate recovers net accumulation. Derivative and integral are inverse stories under suitable conditions.

## Worked intuition

Each thin rectangle $f(x_i)\Delta x$ is a tiny account of accumulation. Add them. Refine the partition. The integral is what the sum is trying to become.

If you have ever approximated distance by reading a speedometer every few seconds and multiplying by time gaps, you have already lived a Riemann sum. Gaps matter. Sign matters if direction reverses. Sparse readings give a rough integral; dense readings give a better one. Calculus names the ideal limit of that refinement.

Density over volume becomes mass. Power over time becomes energy. Loss over a probability distribution becomes expected risk. The pattern is always: local contribution times tiny measure, then sum, then refine.

## Common confusions

**Area always means "positive paint."** Signed integrals record cancellation. Integrating velocity can return displacement (net), not total distance traveled. Total distance needs absolute speed, or careful splitting where the sign changes.

**Antiderivatives without $+C$.** Because constants vanish under differentiation, indefinite integrals name families. Forgetting $+C$ is forgetting that many heights share one slope function.

**"Integral equals antiderivative" without limits.** The definite integral $\int_a^b f$ is a number (net accumulation). An antiderivative is a function. The fundamental theorem connects them: evaluate an antiderivative at the endpoints (under the usual hypotheses).

**Confusing average height with total accumulation.** Average value of $f$ on $[a,b]$ is $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$. The integral itself still has units of $f$ times $x$, not the units of $f$ alone.

**Thinking every integral is geometric area.** Area is the best picture, not the only meaning. Accumulation of rate, mass from density, expectation from weighted loss: same pattern, different units.

**Ignoring units in $\Delta x$.** The thin width carries units. Forget them and the sum's units become mystery meat.

## Real world

Distance from speed over time, total energy from power over time, and total rainfall from rate charts are accumulation problems. Accounting systems that add contributions over bins are discrete cousins of the same idea.

A trip reconstructed from sparse GPS speeds is a Riemann sum in traffic. A continuous model is the dream of infinite refinement. Engineers live between those poles constantly.

## AI / ML bridge

Expected loss (risk) is an accumulation of loss weighted by a data-generating distribution:

$$
R(f) = \mathbb{E}[\ell(y, f(x))]
$$

Training estimates that accumulation from samples: empirical risk is a Monte Carlo stand-in for an integral or sum over the true distribution. Mini-batches are coarser Riemann-like probes of the same landscape.

You will meet expectation formally in probability. Here, notice only that ML's central objective is already an integral in spirit.

## A longer numerical studio

Approximate $\int_0^1 x^2\,dx$ with four rectangles (right endpoints), then eight, then sixteen. Watch the sums climb toward $1/3$. That climb is the integral becoming real.

Now approximate distance from a speed table: speeds $v_i$ at times with gaps $\Delta t_i$. Form $\sum v_i \Delta t_i$. Change the gaps. Notice how sparse sampling lies. This is a Riemann sum in traffic, and it is also how numerical integrators begin.

Signed area studio: integrate a function that crosses zero. Watch positive and negative panels cancel in the net. Then compute total variation of area with absolute values. Net versus total is the displacement-versus-distance distinction in geometric clothes.

## Exhaustion as a virtue

Archimedes trapped areas between controllable shapes and squeezed. The modern integral keeps the squeeze and upgrades the bookkeeping. When a problem feels impossible whole, ask: what thin piece can I control? That question is the Veg-O-Matic ethic without the joke brand.

Grid a lake on a map. Count squares. Refine the grid. You are doing exhaustion with graph paper. Calculus names the ideal limit; your hand already knew the move.

## Extended examples

**Right versus left rectangles.** On an increasing function, right Riemann sums overestimate; left underestimate. Refinement squeezes them together when the integral exists. The squeeze is Archimedes's ethic in modern clothes.

**Average value.** $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$ is the constant height whose rectangle matches the net area. Useful in physics and in "mean temperature over a day" talk.

**Accumulation functions.** $A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ turns a rate into a running total. Differentiating $A$ recovers $f$ under the FTC hypotheses. This is the cleanest handshake picture.

**Discrete logs as cousins.** Summing token counts, rain gauge tips, or battery milliamp-hours is a Riemann sum with stubborn $\Delta x$. Calculus is the continuum dream of those logs.

**Signed panels.** Sketch $f(x)=x$ from $-1$ to $2$. Net area is not the painted absolute area. Choose the quantity your application needs before you integrate.

## Glossary of moves

- **Riemann sum:** weighted heights times widths, then add.
- **Definite integral:** limit of Riemann sums (when it exists).
- **Indefinite integral / antiderivative:** reverse derivative, plus $C$.
- **Signed area:** net accumulation with orientation.
- **FTC:** differentiation and integration as inverse stories.
- **Average value:** integral divided by length of interval.
- **Empirical risk:** sample average standing in for an integral/sum of loss.

## One more vignette

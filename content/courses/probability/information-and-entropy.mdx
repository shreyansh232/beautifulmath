## How this idea was born

Communication through noise forces a hard question: how much **surprise** does a message carry, and how many bits do you need to send it reliably? Compression, phone lines, and codes all trade in uncertainty that can be measured, not only felt.

**Claude Shannon**'s 1948 paper founded information theory: bits, entropy, and communication through noise. MacTutor and the primary paper (linked in further reading) are the stops. Steal the ethic: playful rigor. Treat "information" as something you can define and measure, then build systems that respect the math. Entropy is expected surprisal. Cross-entropy ties that instinct to the losses classifiers use every day.

For a modern rereader, chance gave you weights and expectation gave you averages; this chapter asks how surprised you should be, then averages that surprise. Language models live here.

## First principles

<EntropyBarsDiagram />

If an event has probability $p$, its **surprisal** (self-information) is

$$
I = -\log_2 p
$$

(in bits). Rarer events surprise more.

**Entropy** of a discrete distribution is expected surprisal:

$$
H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i
$$

A sure outcome has entropy 0. A fair coin has entropy 1 bit.

If true labels follow $p$ and a model predicts $q$,

$$
H(p, q) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i
$$

is **cross-entropy**. When $p$ is a one-hot label, this becomes $-\log q_{\text{correct}}$: the usual classifier loss.

**KL divergence** (preview): $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| q) = H(p, q) - H(p)$ measures extra surprise from using $q$ instead of $p$.

## Worked intuition

If a fair coin flip landing heads barely surprises you, a fair die landing on a predicted face surprises you more (probability $1/6$ versus $1/2$). Shannon turned that graded surprise into bits. Entropy averages it. Cross-entropy asks how surprised a model should be by the truth.

English text has lower entropy than random letters: compression exploits that structure. Shannon made the intuition quantitative. Noisy channels need codes that respect entropy limits; that is communication theory's heart.

Algebrica may not center Shannon entropy on one page, but its discrete random variables treatment is the right substrate. Shannon's 1948 paper (in further reading) is worth skimming for definitions alone.

Cross-entropy is merciless toward confident wrongness: if $q_{\text{correct}}$ is tiny, $-\log q_{\text{correct}}$ is huge. That pain is the training signal.

## Common confusions

**Information as "meaning."** Shannon information is about surprise and coding length under a distribution, not about semantic importance. A rare nonsense string can carry many bits.

**Entropy as "disorder" without a distribution.** Entropy is a functional of a probability distribution. Metaphorical disorder talk is optional and often sloppy.

**Cross-entropy versus accuracy.** Accuracy counts winners. Cross-entropy scores probabilistic honesty. A model can be accurate yet poorly calibrated, or the reverse on hard sets.

**KL as a symmetric distance.** $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| q)$ is not generally equal to $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \| p)$, and it is not a metric. It is a directed extra-surprisal score.

**Calling any large file "high entropy" without a model.** Entropy needs a distribution. A large deterministic file can compress to almost nothing.

**Equating mutual information with causation.** Dependence is not cause. Information measures statistical connection, not mechanism.

## Real world
Compression, noisy phone lines, and efficient codes all trade in entropy: how much uncertainty a source has, and how many bits you need. File formats, error-correcting codes, and streaming protocols are engineering descendants of the same ideas.

Whenever a system says "this message was unexpected," Shannon offers a meter.

## AI / ML bridge

Cross-entropy loss is the workhorse of classification. KL divergence measures how one distribution mismatches another. Language models are probability machines scored by information-theoretic losses: next-token training is cross-entropy on steroids.

Training a classifier with cross-entropy pushes $q$ toward $p$. Generative models often minimize divergences between data and model distributions. Calibration and proper scoring rules live in the same neighborhood.

## For the rereader

After Bayes, notice that information gain from an observation relates to how much entropy drops after conditioning. After gradients, notice that $-\log q$ is a smooth, steep penalty near confident mistakes - a loss landscape choice, not an accident.

A sharper nuance: bits versus nats is a base-of-logarithm convention ($\log_2$ versus $\ln$). The geometry is the same up to a constant factor. Papers switch bases casually; keep the factor in mind when comparing numbers.

Also reread the "meaning versus surprise" distinction. Product copy that says "our model has high information" without a distribution is marketing fog. You now have language to demand the distribution.

On a third visit, compute cross-entropy for two fictional classifier outputs on the same one-hot label: one humble, one arrogantly wrong. Feel the pain differential.

Keep a list of compressions you admire (file formats, abbreviations, diagrams). Ask what entropy they exploited.

## A longer surprise studio

Compute surprisal $-\log_2 p$ for $p\in\{1,1/2,1/4,1/8\}$. Watch rare events cost more bits. Then compute entropy of a fair coin versus a coin with $P(H)=0.9$. Certainty shrinks entropy. That shrinkage is why compression loves structure and why a sure label has entropy zero.

## Cross-entropy pain

For a one-hot true class, loss $-\log q_{\text{correct}}$ explodes as $q_{\text{correct}}\to 0$. Plot it mentally. Confident wrongness is expensive. That expense is the training signal's sharpness.

Compare two models with the same accuracy but different probabilities on the correct class. Cross-entropy distinguishes them; accuracy may not. Probabilistic honesty is a different virtue than hard-decision correctness.

## KL as extra surprise

$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=H(p,q)-H(p)$ is extra surprise from using $q$ in place of $p$. It is directed. Swapping $p$ and $q$ can change the number a lot. Do not call it "distance" without caveats.

## Extended examples

**Two-coin entropy.** Fair coin: $H=1$ bit. Biased $0.99$: $H$ near zero. Plot $H$ versus bias mentally; peak at fairness for binary variables.

**Cross-entropy table.** True label class $2$. Model A assigns $0.7$ there; model B assigns $0.2$. Losses $-\log 0.7$ versus $-\log 0.2$. Pain differential is the lesson.

**Compression link.** A file of a million zeros compresses absurdly well. Entropy of the source is low under a model that expects zeros. Random bytes do not.

**KL asymmetry toy.** Let $p$ be fair coin, $q$ nearly sure heads. Compare $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$. Feel the directionality.

**Language model glimpse.** Next-token training minimizes cross-entropy of predicted token distributions versus data. This chapter is the scorekeeper's manual.

## Glossary of moves

- **Surprisal:** $-\log p$; rarer costs more.
- **Entropy:** expected surprisal.
- **Cross-entropy:** expected surprisal under a model's $q$.
- **KL divergence:** extra surprisal of using $q$ instead of $p$.
- **Bit / nat:** log base convention.
- **Calibration cousin:** probabilistic honesty beyond hard accuracy.
- **Compression:** exploit low entropy structure.

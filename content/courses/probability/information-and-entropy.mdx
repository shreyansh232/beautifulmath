## First principles

If an event has probability $p$, its **surprisal** (self-information) is

$$
I = -\log_2 p
$$

(in bits). Rarer events surprise more.

**Entropy** of a discrete distribution is expected surprisal:

$$
H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i
$$

A sure outcome has entropy 0. A fair coin has entropy 1 bit.


## Slow down: surprise has weight

If a fair coin flip landing heads barely surprises you, a fair die landing on a predicted face surprises you more. Shannon turned that graded surprise into bits. Entropy averages it. Cross-entropy asks how surprised a model should be by the truth.

## Cross-entropy

If true labels follow $p$ and a model predicts $q$,

$$
H(p, q) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i
$$

When $p$ is a one-hot label, this becomes $-\log q_{\text{correct}}$: the classifier loss.

## KL divergence (preview)

$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| q) = H(p, q) - H(p)$ measures extra surprise from using $q$ instead of $p$.

## Real world

English text has lower entropy than random letters: compression exploits that structure. Shannon made that intuition quantitative.

## AI / ML bridge

Training a classifier with cross-entropy pushes $q$ toward $p$. Generative models and language models are probability machines scored by information-theoretic losses.

## How this idea was born

Expectation summarizes a single random variable. Life immediately asks about **pairs**: when $X$ runs high, does $Y$ tend to follow? A second human need arrives with every dataset: your estimate is from a **sample**, not the oracle distribution. A third need is uniquely modern in ML dashboards: when a model says "$80\%$," does the world agree?

**Francis Galton** (1822-1911) helped push regression and correlation into quantitative scientific practice; MacTutor sketches the career. **Karl Pearson** later standardized the product-moment correlation coefficient widely used today; MacTutor's Pearson biography covers that biometric program. Steal Galton's measuring instinct carefully, and keep the moral warning loud: association is not automatic causation. (Galton's broader historical legacy is complicated; this chapter borrows the measurement ethic, not a sanitized hero poster.)

For a modern rereader, covariance/correlation, sampling uncertainty, and calibration are three instruments on one workbench.

## First principles

<CalibrationDiagram />

For random variables $X,Y$ with finite means and variances,

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)],
$$

$$
\mathrm{Corr}(X,Y)=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\in[-1,1]
$$

(when $\sigma_X\sigma_Y>0$). Correlation scales covariance to a unitless linear-association score. $\pm 1$ signals perfect linear relationship almost surely; $0$ means uncorrelated, **not** necessarily independent.

**Sampling uncertainty:** an estimate $\hat\rho$ or $\bar X$ from $n$ draws wobbles. Larger $n$ typically tightens the wobble (under standard assumptions), but dependence, bias, and heavy tails can spoil naive confidence. Always ask what was sampled and how.

**Calibration** of probabilities: among cases where a model predicts probability near $p$, the observed frequency should be near $p$ if the model is calibrated. Reliability diagrams bucket predictions and compare forecast average to outcome rate. Sharpness (confident predictions) without calibration is stylish false precision.

## Worked intuition

Covariance is an expected product of centered deviations: same-direction wanderings push it positive; opposite wanderings push it negative. Correlation divides out scales so meters and millimeters can talk.

Sampling is the humility layer. A correlation of $0.4$ on $n=20$ noisy points is a rumor; on $n=10^5$ carefully collected points it is a stronger claim (still not causation).

Calibration is keeping promises about frequencies. Weather forecasts taught this publicly; classifiers must relearn it whenever they optimize a proxy loss that does not enforce reliability.

## Common confusions

**Uncorrelated implies independent.** False in general. Independence implies uncorrelated (when moments exist); the converse needs extra structure ( jointly Gaussian is the famous friendly case).

**Correlation implies causation.** The slogan is famous because the mistake is common. Confounders and selection create dances without direct arrows.

**Calibration equals accuracy.** A model can be calibrated and still useless (always predict the base rate). A model can be accurate on hard labels yet miscalibrated on probabilities.

**Bigger sample fixes bias.** Sample size fights variance; bias from bad sampling frames needs design fixes.

**$|\rho|$ near 1 always means "strong for my task."** Linear association can be strong while the relationship you care about is nonlinear or conditional.

**Test-set metrics without sampling talk.** Every reported AUC/accuracy is an estimate. Especially fragile on small or shifting populations.

## History & stories

Galton's program made co-relation a scientific habit; Pearson's product-moment coefficient standardized a workhorse formula; later statistics deepened sampling distributions and errors-in-variables. Calibration as a named ML concern rides on older meteorological and probabilistic forecasting ethics: stated probabilities should mean something in frequency.

## Real world
Doctors reading risk scores need calibration, not only ranking. Marketers correlating ad spend with sales must hunt confounders (seasonality). Pollsters live and die by sampling frames.

A forecast office that says "$70\%$ rain" on many days should see rain on about $70\%$ of those days. That is the public face of this chapter.

## AI / ML bridge

Modern classifiers often emit scores that need temperature scaling or isotonic regression to improve calibration after training. Expected calibration error (ECE) is a common summary (imperfect, bucket-dependent). Feature correlations drive multicollinearity in linear models and redundancy in representations. Finite test sets make every leaderboard number a random variable.

Honest scope: perfect calibration everywhere is rare under shift. Still, unchecked confidence is a known failure mode in deployed systems. Ranking metrics (AUC) can look fine while probabilities are lying - know which question you asked.

A sharper nuance: sample correlation has a skewed sampling distribution near $\pm 1$. Fisher transformations exist for a reason; rule-of-thumb error bars mislead at the edges.

## Correlation studio

Plot points on a line with tiny noise: $\rho\approx 1$. Plot a circle cloud: $\rho\approx 0$ despite structure. Linear meters miss circular dependence. That picture should stay glued to every correlation report.

## Sampling studio

Draw $n=5$ samples from a fair coin and estimate $p$. Repeat mentally. Feel the wobble. Now $n=500$. Same estimator, different humility.

## Calibration studio

Bucket predicted probabilities: $[0.0,0.1), [0.1,0.2), \ldots$. In each bucket, compare mean prediction to mean outcome. Points on the diagonal are calibrated. Above or below is systematic lie.

## Extended examples

**Spurious seasonal correlation.** Ice cream sales and drowning incidents rise together via summer. Correlation true; causal arrow elsewhere.

**Class imbalance.** Always predicting $P=0.01$ in a $1\%$ positive world can be calibrated in a coarse sense and still miss ranking quality. Separate questions.

**Bootstrap glimpse.** Resample your dataset to see metric wobble. It is not magic, but it makes sampling uncertainty visceral.

**Multicollinearity.** Two features with $|\rho|\approx 1$ make linear coefficients unstable. The joint predictive power may still be fine; interpretation of single coefficients is not.

**ML monitoring.** Track calibration drift after deployment. Populations shift; yesterday's reliability diagram expires.

## Glossary of moves

- **Covariance:** expected product of centered deviations.
- **Correlation:** scaled linear association in $[-1,1]$.
- **Sampling uncertainty:** estimator wobble from finite data.
- **Calibration:** predicted probabilities match outcome frequencies.

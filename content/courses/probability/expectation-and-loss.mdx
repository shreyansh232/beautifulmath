## How this idea was born

Under risk, people need **averages that respect chance**: what an insurer should expect to pay, what a fair game should return, what a planner should treat as a center of mass when outcomes scatter. Probability weights outcomes. Expectation turns those weights into a single summary that training algorithms, insurers, and planners optimize against.

**Andrey Kolmogorov** axiomatized probability in 1933, giving the subject a measure-theoretic spine so expectation and dependence could live in one rigorous house. MacTutor is the short stop. Steal the ethic: pin down the rules so creativity does not collapse into confusion. Loss functions turn prediction mistakes into numbers; **risk** is expected loss. This chapter is where chance becomes an objective.

For a modern rereader, remember that expectation is a summary, not a destiny, and not always a value the random variable can take. Variance and ruin still matter.

## First principles

For a discrete random variable $X$ with values $x_i$ and probabilities $p_i$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i x_i p_i
$$

It is the center of mass of the distribution.

<DistributionDiagram />

Linearity: $\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y]$ always (no independence needed). That convenience is almost unfairly useful.

Variance: $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2]$ measures spread around the center.

A loss $\ell(y, \hat{y})$ scores a mistake. **Risk** is $\mathbb{E}[\ell(Y, f(X))]$. Machine learning estimates risk with an average over samples (empirical risk).

## Worked intuition

A lottery with tiny chance of a huge prize can have a shiny expected value and still be a bad personal plan - variance and ruin matter too. Expectation is a summary, not a destiny, and not always a value the random variable can take (the average of a fair die is $3.5$).

Casinos set rules so their expectation is positive. Insurance prices premiums around expected claims plus margin. Waiting-time models use expectation as a planning number with variance as a honesty check.

In ML, choosing a loss is choosing which mistakes hurt in the average that training will minimize. MSE, cross-entropy, hinge: different geometries of error.

Algebrica's mean-or-expected-value page develops discrete and continuous cases.

Strogatz's dating (secretary) problem shows expectation as strategy: interview candidates in random order, reject the first roughly $1/e$ as calibration, then take the next who beats all of them. Expectation is not only average payoff; it can tell you when to stop searching.

## Common confusions

**Expectation as a typical outcome.** Means can sit where no probability mass lives. Modes and medians answer different questions.

**Linearity requiring independence.** Linearity of expectation holds without independence. Variance calculations do not get the same free pass.

**Empirical risk as the true risk.** The average on a sample is a stand-in. Generalization asks whether that stand-in tracks the expectation under the true distribution. Overfitting is the gap becoming a chasm.

**Ignoring tails while optimizing means.** Fat-tailed losses can make mean-focused training brittle. Companion metrics matter.

**Optimizing average reward while ignoring ruin.** A strategy with positive expectation can still bankrupt you via variance. Kelly-style thinking exists because means are incomplete.

**Assuming empirical risk decreases imply true risk decreases.** Overfitting is the counterexample industry.

## Real world

Insurance premiums, expected wait times, and average damage under risk scenarios are expectations. Project planning that quotes only means without spreads is incomplete communication.

A fair game has expected net gain near zero. Real casinos and real markets rarely offer you that courtesy.

## AI / ML bridge

Training minimizes empirical risk: average loss on data, a stand-in for expected loss under the true distribution. Optimization (calculus) + linear maps (linear algebra) + expectation (this lesson) meet in training.

Stochastic gradient descent estimates $\nabla$ of an expectation with mini-batch averages: chance and calculus shaking hands every step.

## A longer center-of-mass studio

Place point masses on a number line at $1,2,6$ with weights $0.5,0.25,0.25$. Compute the expectation as the balance point. Move the mass at $6$ farther to $20$ with tiny weight $0.05$ (renormalize). Watch the mean chase the outlier while the median resists. Means are sensitive; choose losses knowing that.

## Secretary problem as strategy

Strogatz's "All About e" includes the dating/secretary rule of thumb involving $1/e$. Treat it as a curiosity about expectation meeting stopping rules, not as dating advice. The mathematical thrill is that an optimal-stopping threshold can be expressed with $e$. Expectation here is strategy, not only average payoff.

## Loss geometries

Squared error punishes large residuals harshly. Absolute error is more median-friendly. Cross-entropy (next chapter) punishes confident wrong probabilities. Choosing $\ell$ is choosing which mistakes should dominate the average that training minimizes.

## Extended examples

**Die mean.** $\mathbb{E}=3.5$ for a fair six-sided die. No face shows $3.5$. Expectation need not be attainable.

**Linearity demo.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be indicators of dependent events. Still $\mathbb{E}[X+Y]=\mathbb{E}X+\mathbb{E}Y$. Dependence can wait for variance.

**Loss choice.** On targets with outliers, compare training with squared versus absolute loss in a tiny spreadsheet. Watch which fit chases outliers.

**Empirical risk curve.** Plot average training loss versus epoch beside a validation average. The gap is the generalization drama in one figure.

**Casino edge.** A game with $\mathbb{E}[\text{net}]=-0.01$ per play is a business. Repeated play makes the law of large numbers a weapon against you.

## Glossary of moves

- **Expectation:** probability-weighted average / center of mass.
- **Linearity:** $\mathbb{E}[aX+bY]=a\mathbb{E}X+b\mathbb{E}Y$ always.
- **Variance:** expected squared deviation.
- **Loss:** scores a prediction mistake.
- **Risk:** expected loss.
- **Empirical risk:** average loss on samples.
- **Proper summary:** mean vs median depends on loss geometry.

## One more vignette

Casinos do not need every guest to lose tonight. They need the expectation to lean their way across the crowd and the week. ML training is a cousin: it needs average loss to lean downward across batches and epochs. Variance still decides whether your particular path feels like doom. Means without spreads are incomplete weather reports.
